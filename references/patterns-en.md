# English patterns

Patterns are numbered strongest and most frequent first. EN-1 to EN-5 justify an edit on one sighting. Patterns marked *weak alone* count only when other tells share the passage.

## A. Staging instead of stating

### EN-1. Not X but Y

**Watch for:** "it's not X, it's Y"; "not just / not only / not merely X, but Y"; "X rather than Y" used for weight; the contrast split over two sentences ("This isn't about X. It's about Y."); a clipped tail ("..., no guesswork").
**Fix:** The negative half denies something nobody said, so the positive half sounds bigger. State the positive claim. Keep a contrast only when the reader really believes the negative half, or when both halves carry information.
**Before:**
> This isn't just a calendar app. It's a new way to own your time.
**After:**
> The app is a calendar that blocks out focus time automatically.

### EN-2. One-line closers and dramatic fragments

**Watch for:** a one-sentence paragraph that repeats the paragraph above; "That's the point." "Let that sink in." "Read that again."; a line after an example that explains the example ("That's the power of habit."); a row of fragments ("No meetings. No email. Just work."); "every. single. day."
**Fix:** Cut a closer that repeats or explains. Keep it if it adds a new fact. Merge fragments into one sentence with a specific claim.
**Before:**
> We cut the build from 14 minutes to 3. Three minutes. That's the difference between waiting and shipping.
**After:**
> We cut the build from 14 minutes to 3, so people stopped batching their changes.

(The "so" clause must come from the source. If it doesn't, end after "3".)

### EN-3. Sayings that sound deep

**Watch for:** at its core, the real question is, what truly matters, fundamentally, the heart of the matter; "X is the new Y"; "X is not a tool, it's a mirror"; "the currency of", "the language of".
**Fix:** Replace the saying with the specific claim under it.
**Before:**
> At its core, onboarding is the language of trust.
**After:**
> New hires who get a working laptop and a buddy on day one tend to stay longer.

(Only if the source says so. Otherwise: "Onboarding affects whether new hires stay.")

### EN-4. Staged run-up before the point

**Watch for:** Let's dive in, let's break it down, here's what you need to know, without further ado, Here's the thing, Honestly?, Look, Real talk, Spoiler:
**Fix:** Delete the run-up and start with the point. "Honestly" inside a casual sentence is fine; the tell is the stand-alone opener.
**Before:**
> Should you upgrade? Honestly? Here's the thing: it depends on your plan.
**After:**
> Whether to upgrade depends on your plan.

### EN-5. Arguing with no one

**Watch for:** I'm not saying, to be clear, don't get me wrong, this isn't about, some might argue, a tempting approach would be, you might think... but.
**Fix:** Remove the objection or rejected option that nobody raised. Keep an objection the text names and answers, or an option a reader would really consider.
**Before:**
> To be clear, I'm not saying remote work is bad. A tempting approach would be to ban it, but we chose to set two office days.
**After:**
> We set two office days a week.

## B. Rhythm by rule

### EN-6. Forced triads

**Fix:** Use as many items as the meaning has. Three parallel examples followed by a lesson is the same tell at paragraph scale.
**Before:**
> The course builds confidence, clarity, and community.
**After:**
> The course ends with a mock interview, which most students say helped most.

(Use only the items the source supports.)

### EN-7. Repeated sentence openings

**Fix:** Merge sentences or vary the subject. Deliberate repetition for rhythm is fine.
**Before:**
> He opened the file. He checked the logs. He found the bug.
**After:**
> He opened the file, checked the logs, and found the bug.

### EN-8. Dashes everywhere

**Rule:** The final text has no em dashes (—), en dashes used as dashes (–), or ` -- ` unless the writer's sample uses them; then match the sample's rate. Use a comma, period, colon, or parentheses. Leave code, paths, and URLs alone. One dash is *weak alone*.

### EN-9. Stacked qualifiers *(weak alone)*

**Watch for:** could potentially, may possibly, arguably might, it's worth noting that it could.
**Fix:** Keep the one qualifier the source supports.

### EN-10. Hidden actors *(weak alone)*

**Fix:** Name who does what when it helps. "No setup required" becomes "You don't need to set anything up."

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

### EN-11. Overused AI words

**Watch for:** delve, tapestry, testament, landscape (abstract), showcase, underscore (verb), pivotal, crucial, robust (figurative), seamless, elevate, leverage (verb), unlock, empower, foster, navigate (figurative), realm, intricate, meticulous, vibrant, game-changer, additionally.
**Fix:** Use the plain word. One formal word is not a tell; a cluster is.

### EN-12. Inflated significance

**Watch for:** marks a pivotal moment, plays a key role, a testament to, sets the stage for, lasting legacy, in today's fast-paced world, the future looks bright, exciting times ahead; a "Challenges and Future Outlook" section.
**Fix:** Keep the fact, drop the significance, and end on the last concrete fact.
**Before:**
> The café opened in 2019, marking a pivotal moment for the neighborhood's coffee scene. The future looks bright.
**After:**
> The café opened in 2019.

### EN-13. Sales language

**Watch for:** nestled, in the heart of, breathtaking, stunning, must-visit, hidden gem, world-class, unparalleled, rich heritage, curated.
**Fix:** Say what the thing is and where.

### EN-14. Borrowed authority

**Watch for:** experts say, studies show, many believe, industry leaders agree, featured in [list of outlets].
**Fix:** Name the source and what it said, from the text. If the text has no source, cut the claim or soften it to what is known.

### EN-15. Empty -ing riders

**Watch for:** ..., highlighting / reflecting / showcasing / ensuring / fostering / underscoring ... at the end of a sentence.
**Fix:** Cut the rider unless the source supports what it claims.

### EN-16. Avoiding "is" and "has"

**Watch for:** serves as, stands as, functions as, boasts, features, offers (for "has").
**Fix:** Use is, are, has.

## D. Formatting by rule

### EN-17. Bold labels and decorative bold

**Fix:** Remove bold that marks nothing. A list where every item is "**Label:** sentence repeating the label" becomes a sentence.

### EN-18. Decorative headings

**Fix:** Sentence case. No emojis or arrows in headings or list items. No rule between every section. Name what the section holds, not a slogan.

### EN-19. Curly quotes *(weak alone)*

**Fix:** Use the quote style the target format uses.

## E. Leftovers from the chat

### EN-20. Chatbot residue

**Watch for:** Great question!, Certainly!, Of course!, I hope this helps, Let me know if..., Would you like me to..., Here's a...
**Fix:** Remove the wrapper and keep the content.

### EN-21. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses

**Watch for:** as of my last update, based on available information, details are limited, likely grew up, it is believed that.
**Fix:** Say what the source does not show, or cut the sentence. Never keep the guess.

### EN-22. Writing about the document

**Watch for:** "this section covers", "the table below shows", "this function was added to replace", "compiled from".
**Fix:** Describe the subject, not the page. Keep a convention only when the reader cannot see it.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

### EN-23. Re-explaining what the reader knows

Applies to replies in a thread, chat, or email chain.
**Fix:** Lead with the decision or answer. Keep only the one fact the reader lacks and any link they need. The full diagnosis belongs in a ticket or document.
